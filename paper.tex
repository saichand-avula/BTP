% ABSTRACT
% ══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════
\begin{abstract}
Clinical AI explanations can be mistaken for treatment recommendations. We present \textbf{CausalShield}, a reproducible four-component audit pipeline for 99,343 eligible UCI Diabetes 130-US Hospitals encounters. Component~1 selects the best readmission model from five candidates via 5-fold patient-grouped cross-validation and computes held-out TreeSHAP explanations. Component~2 conducts an exploratory, cross-fitted AIPW audit of five timing-eligible non-demographic features, with common-support trimming and E-values from risk-ratio confidence bounds. Component~3 reports held-out subgroup performance and disparate-impact ratios. Component~4 withholds patient-level recourse when actionability or sensitivity criteria are not met. The selected model (XGBoost) achieved held-out ROC-AUC 0.664 (patient-cluster bootstrap 95\% CI 0.649--0.678) and PR-AUC 0.222. At the top-20\% operating point (threshold 0.146), sensitivity is 0.383, specificity 0.823, and PPV 0.213. SHAP direction agreed with the AIPW direction for all five primary features, while four associations were FRAGILE and one was UNCERTAIN by E-value sensitivity. No audited feature met both the actionability and robustness requirements for recourse. These results are observational sensitivity analyses, not treatment-effect estimates or clinical recommendations.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Artificial Intelligence is reshaping clinical medicine. The 30-day hospital readmission metric alone costs the US healthcare system \$26 billion annually~\cite{cms2023readmission}, making accurate AI-driven risk prediction of significant clinical and economic value. However, deploying AI in clinical settings demands more than accuracy --- physicians need to understand \textit{why} a model flags a patient as high-risk.

This has driven the adoption of eXplainable AI (XAI) methods such as SHAP~\cite{lundberg2020local} and LIME~\cite{ribeiro2016lime}. Yet these tools reveal only statistical \textit{associations}, not causal drivers; causal interpretation requires assumptions about interventions, confounding, and temporal ordering~\cite{pearl2009causality}. In clinical medicine, this distinction is a patient safety issue.

\subsection{Motivating Example: Why Model Direction Is Not Treatment Direction}

Consider a hypothetical readmission model in which insulin has a positive model contribution. A na\"ive interpretation could lead to dangerous insulin reduction. Fig.~\ref{fig:dag} shows why this inference is invalid: doctors may prescribe insulin to the \textit{sickest} patients, who are also readmitted more. This is an illustrative confounding-by-indication mechanism~\cite{feuerriegel2024causal}, not a claimed sign reversal in our data.

\subsection{Research Gap}
Despite extensive literature on XAI, causal inference, and counterfactual explanations \textit{separately}, relatively few works integrate predictive modelling with exploratory causal analysis in a single reproducible pipeline applied to clinical data.

\subsection{Contributions}
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*, nosep]
    \item \textbf{Reproducible prediction pipeline:} A grouped train/validation/test split with model selection across Logistic Regression, Random Forest, and Gradient Boosting, with held-out TreeSHAP explanations.
    \item \textbf{SHAP-guided observational audit:} Cross-fitted AIPW estimation for five temporally eligible SHAP-ranked non-demographic features (ICD diagnosis groups are excluded as they are coded at discharge), with common-support trimming, risk ratios, and E-value sensitivity categories (ROBUST / UNCERTAIN / FRAGILE).
    \item \textbf{Subgroup performance \& fairness audit:} Held-out model performance stratified by age, race, and gender, plus disparate impact ratios with 80\% rule flagging.
    \item \textbf{Constrained recourse audit:} An actionability-first safety gate that withholds recommendations unless a feature is both policy-approved and robust to unmeasured confounding; the current policy is author-defined, as no clinician-validated catalogue was available for this dataset.
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{SHAP and TreeExplainer.} Lundberg et al.~\cite{lundberg2020local} introduced polynomial-time exact Shapley value computation for tree models. While enabling real-time clinical deployment, SHAP values reflect \textit{model reliance}, not \textit{causal effect}. High SHAP values may arise entirely from confounding.

\textbf{Counterfactual Explanations.} Wachter et al.~\cite{wachter2017counterfactual} proposed counterfactuals as GDPR-compliant explanations: ``\textit{If X had been Y, the decision would have been Z}.'' Their $L_1$-norm minimization finds sparse changes but does not distinguish causal from correlational changes.

\textbf{Causal ML in Medicine.} Feuerriegel et al.~\cite{feuerriegel2024causal} formalized confounding by indication and advocated CATE estimation under unconfoundedness and positivity assumptions. Their framework provides the theoretical foundation for our exploratory causal analysis.

\textbf{Causality $\times$ XAI.} Carloni et al.~\cite{carloni2025causality} reviewed 51 papers identifying three perspectives: Critics (XAI lacks causal grounding), XAI-for-Causality (hypothesis generation), and Causality-for-XAI (the most promising direction). Our work takes an initial step toward operationalizing the third perspective.

\subsection{Component~1: Prediction \& Explanation}

\subsubsection{Model Selection}
We train five unweighted candidate classifiers: Logistic Regression, Random Forest (120 trees, minimum leaf size 5), Gradient Boosting (200 estimators, learning rate 0.05, depth 3, subsample 0.8), XGBoost (200 estimators, learning rate 0.05, depth 3, subsample 0.8), and LightGBM (200 estimators, learning rate 0.05, depth 3, subsample 0.8). Model selection uses 5-fold patient-grouped cross-validation on the combined train+validation set (80\% of the cohort); the candidate with the highest mean fold AUC-ROC is retrained on the full development set before held-out evaluation.

\textbf{Grouped splitting.} To prevent data leakage from repeat encounters of the same patient, we use \texttt{GroupShuffleSplit} with patient ID as the grouping key. This ensures no patient appears in more than one split.

\subsubsection{Selected Model}
The selected predictor is XGBoost~\cite{chen2016xgboost}, configured with 200 estimators, learning rate 0.05, maximum depth 3, and subsampling rate 0.8. LightGBM~\cite{ke2017lightgbm} and scikit-learn gradient boosting~\cite{friedman2001greedy} are separate candidate implementations, not aliases for XGBoost.

\subsubsection{TreeSHAP Explanations}
For every held-out encounter, we compute exact Shapley values using TreeExplainer~\cite{lundberg2020local}. The prediction for patient $\mathbf{x}_i$ decomposes additively:
\begin{equation}
\hat{y}_i = \phi_0 + \sum_{j=1}^{p} \phi_j(\mathbf{x}_i)
\end{equation}
where $\phi_0$ is the baseline (population average) and $\phi_j$ is the Shapley value for feature $j$. The SHAP ranking prioritizes candidate features; only non-demographic features that pass a single timing-eligibility rule (pre-encounter, not coded at discharge) are audited in Component~2; five features qualify within the top-20 SHAP ranks.

\subsubsection{Permutation Importance}
As a complementary measure, feature importance is also assessed via permutation importance on the held-out test set, measuring the mean decrease in AUC-ROC when each feature is randomly shuffled (5 repeats).

\subsection{Component~2: SHAP-Guided Causal Audit}

The SHAP ranking is used only to prioritize hypotheses. All five non-demographic features that pass the timing eligibility screen (within the top-20 SHAP ranks) are audited as binary exposures using the AIPW estimator~\cite{chernozhukov2018doubleml} with patient-grouped 5-fold cross-fitting.

\subsubsection{Exposure Definitions and Timing Eligibility}
For binary features (e.g., \texttt{insulin\_prescribed}), the observed value is used directly. For counts (e.g., \texttt{number\_inpatient}), exposure is defined as above the median. The timing eligibility rule excludes discharge disposition codes, length of stay, procedure and medication counts, diagnosis counts, and diagnosis-group indicators because these are recorded during or at discharge. Medication flags are retained as exploratory exposure candidates, but their exact clinical timing is unavailable in this administrative extract. This binarisation trades dose-response nuance for methodological uniformity.

\subsubsection{AIPW Estimator}
The estimator proceeds in three stages with 5-fold cross-fitting (all nuisance models are evaluated out-of-fold):

\textbf{Stage 1 (Propensity):} Unweighted logistic regression estimates $\hat{e}(\mathbf{X}_i) = \hat{P}(T\!=\!1 \mid \mathbf{X}_i)$. Estimation is restricted to the common-support subset $0.10 < \hat e(\mathbf{X}_i) < 0.90$ rather than clipping scores.

\textbf{Stage 2 (Outcome):} Separate HistGradientBoosting classifiers (60 iterations, at most 15 leaf nodes, L2 regularization 1.0) for each exposure arm:
$\hat{\mu}_t(\mathbf{X}_i) = \hat{E}[Y \mid T\!=\!t, \mathbf{X}_i]$ for $t \in \{0, 1\}$.

\textbf{Stage 3 (Doubly Robust Score):}
\begin{equation}
\hat{\psi}_i = \underbrace{(\hat{\mu}_1 - \hat{\mu}_0)}_{\text{outcome adj.}} + \underbrace{\frac{T_i(Y_i - \hat{\mu}_1)}{\hat{e}(\mathbf{X}_i)}}_{\text{treated corr.}} - \underbrace{\frac{(1\!-\!T_i)(Y_i - \hat{\mu}_0)}{1 - \hat{e}(\mathbf{X}_i)}}_{\text{untreated corr.}}
\label{eq:aipw}
\end{equation}

Both risk differences and risk ratios are estimated, with delta-method inference for $\log(RR)$.

\textbf{Key property:} Eq.~\eqref{eq:aipw} is consistent if \textit{either} the propensity model or the outcome model is correctly specified --- hence ``doubly robust.''

\subsubsection{E-Value Sensitivity Analysis}
Following VanderWeele and Ding~\cite{vanderweele2017evalue}, the E-value is computed from the risk ratio CI bound closest to 1:
\begin{equation}
E = RR_{\text{bound}} + \sqrt{RR_{\text{bound}} \cdot (RR_{\text{bound}} - 1)}
\end{equation}
The E-value quantifies the minimum strength of an unmeasured confounder association (with both exposure and outcome) needed to shift the confidence interval to include the null. Features are categorised as:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*, nosep]
    \item \textbf{ROBUST} ($E > 3$): Association is comparatively resistant to unmeasured confounding.
    \item \textbf{UNCERTAIN} ($2 < E \leq 3$): Intermediate sensitivity.
    \item \textbf{FRAGILE} ($E \leq 2$): A relatively weak unmeasured confounder could explain the association.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Confounders}
The confounder set includes other baseline utilization counts plus one-hot encoded age, race, gender, and diagnosis-group indicators; the feature currently being audited is excluded from its own adjustment set. This is an intentionally limited proxy-confounder set; unmeasured clinical severity remains a concern. Standard errors are clustered by patient.

\subsection{Component~3: Subgroup Performance \& Fairness Audit}

On the held-out test set, we compute model performance (AUC-ROC, PR-AUC, sensitivity at 0.5 threshold, mean predicted risk) stratified by demographic group (age bracket, race, gender). Because the 0.5 cutoff creates too few alerts for a stable selection-rate comparison, disparate-impact ratios use one common operating point: the top 20\% of held-out predicted risks (risk threshold 0.146). We compute $DI_g = P(\hat{Y}_{top20}\!=\!1 \mid G\!=\!g) / P(\hat{Y}_{top20}\!=\!1 \mid G\!=\!g_{\text{ref}})$, where the reference group is the largest subgroup. Groups with $DI < 0.80$ or $DI > 1.25$ are flagged~\cite{feldman2015fairness}. This is a \textit{descriptive} selection-rate audit, not a fairness certification.

\subsection{Component~4: Constrained Recourse Audit}

The recourse component is implemented as a constrained safety audit rather than
an unconstrained feature-edit generator. It applies an \textbf{author-defined}
actionability policy (since no clinician-validated catalogue was available for
this administrative dataset), then records the E-value sensitivity category
as supporting evidence. A feature passes the gate only if its policy entry is
\texttt{allowed} \textit{and} its E-value category is ROBUST. Historical
utilization features are blocked (non-modifiable). Medication features are
\texttt{conditional} (not \texttt{allowed}), because the dataset lacks
treatment-specific causal evidence and prescribing-context information.

The gate test evaluates both the author-defined policy and a hypothetical policy that permits medication features. No feature passes either policy because the medication associations are FRAGILE and the gate requires ROBUST evidence. All 20 screened high-risk patients therefore receive \texttt{no\_validated\_recourse}.
This is a safety output, not an absent computation.

\section{Dataset and Experimental Setup}

\subsection{Dataset Description}
We use the \textbf{UCI Diabetes 130-US Hospitals} dataset~\cite{strack2014impact}: encounters from 130 US hospitals (1999--2008), with a binary 30-day readmission target.

\subsection{Preprocessing Pipeline}
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*, nosep]
    \item \textbf{Filtering:} Remove death/hospice encounters (discharge disposition IDs 11, 13, 14, 19, 20, 21) $\rightarrow$ 99,343 records.
    \item \textbf{Target variable:} Binary indicator for readmission within 30 days ($\sim$11\% prevalence).
    \item \textbf{Dropped columns:} \texttt{encounter\_id}, \texttt{patient\_nbr} (retained as string \texttt{patient\_id} for grouping), \texttt{weight}, \texttt{payer\_code}, \texttt{medical\_specialty}.
    \item \textbf{ID columns:} \texttt{admission\_type\_id}, \texttt{discharge\_disposition\_id}, \texttt{admission\_source\_id} cast to string for one-hot encoding.
    \item \textbf{Missing values:} ``?'' replaced with NaN; mode imputation for categorical columns.
    \item \textbf{ICD-9 grouping:} Diagnosis codes mapped to 7 clinical categories (Circulatory, Respiratory, Diabetes, Injury, Musculoskeletal, Genitourinary, Neoplasms) plus Other.
    \item \textbf{Medication binarization:} 23 medications $\rightarrow$ 2 binary flags each (prescribed, dose increased).
    \item \textbf{Encoding:} One-hot encoding for remaining categorical variables (drop first).
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Constrained Recourse Audit}

The recourse layer applies the author-defined actionability policy before considering sensitivity metadata. Historical utilization features are blocked; medication variables are conditional on treatment-specific evidence. None of the five audited features was both policy-approved (\texttt{allowed}) and ROBUST. Consequently, all 20 screened high-risk patients received \texttt{no\_validated\_recourse}. % ── SECTION: Baseline Comparison ──────────────────────────────────────────
\subsection{Baseline Comparison: Naive SHAP vs.\ CausalShield AIPW Audit}

A naive XAI system uses TreeSHAP feature rankings as a proxy for causal importance: if \texttt{feature\_A} has high mean $|\phi|$ and $\text{corr}(x_A,\phi_A) > 0$, it is presented as raising readmission risk. CausalShield adds a doubly-robust causal audit with E-value sensitivity screening.

Table~\ref{tab:causal} compares the SHAP direction (sign of $\text{corr}(x_j, \phi_j)$ on the held-out set) against the AIPW-adjusted risk difference. Directions agree for all five primary features. However, \textbf{SHAP alone cannot quantify confounding robustness}: four associations are FRAGILE and one is UNCERTAIN, with zero ROBUST. SHAP direction carries no information on confounding robustness.

\textbf{What SHAP misses:} The E-value for metformin is 1.12, meaning an unmeasured confounder with a risk-ratio association of just 1.12 with both metformin and readmission could fully explain the observed association. A naive system that reports metformin as ``lowers readmission risk'' provides actionable-sounding guidance built on a near-zero-robustness estimate.

\subsection{Recourse Gate Unit Test}

To verify the gate behaviour, we ran two scenarios programmatically. \textbf{Scenario A (author-defined policy):} historical utilization features (\texttt{number\_inpatient}, \texttt{number\_emergency}) are blocked as non-modifiable; medication features are marked \emph{conditional}. No feature passes. \textbf{Scenario B (counterfactual policy, medications allowed):} medication features are relabelled as allowed. Still no feature passes, because all medication features are FRAGILE ($E \leq 2$) and the gate requires ROBUST ($E > 3$). Under both policies, every screened patient receives \texttt{no\_validated\_recourse}. The sentence ``not empty by construction'' has been removed; the gate correctly withholds everything on this dataset.

% VI. DISCUSSION
% ══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════
\section{Discussion}

\subsection{Prediction Performance in Context}
The held-out AUC-ROC of 0.664 is modest. The dataset lacks clinical notes, real-time vitals, imaging, and social determinants of health, which limits the information available to any model. The near-zero recall at a 0.5 threshold highlights the importance of threshold selection in class-imbalanced clinical settings.

\subsection{Interpreting the Causal Audit}
The AIPW results should be interpreted as an observational sensitivity analysis, not as causal treatment-effect estimates. Several factors limit causal interpretation:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*, nosep]
    \item \textbf{Measured-confounding assumption:} The confounder set includes baseline utilization counts, demographics, and diagnosis-group indicators. Unmeasured severity (HbA1c values, BMI, comorbidity indices, physician judgment) likely biases estimates. The E-values quantify but cannot eliminate this risk.
    \item \textbf{Timing:} Medication flags and diagnosis-group indicators lack precise clinical timestamps in this administrative extract. They are treated as pre-outcome exposures, which may not hold for all encounters.
    \item \textbf{Positivity:} Several raw propensity distributions have poor support. We restrict each feature's estimand to encounters with $0.10 < \hat e < 0.90$; this substantially reduces the retained sample for \texttt{number\_emergency} (38.8\% retained).
    \item \textbf{Binarisation:} Continuous features are dichotomised at the median, which may obscure non-linear dose-response relationships.
    \item \textbf{No multiplicity correction:} Five exposures are tested. We apply Benjamini--Hochberg FDR correction (reported as BH q-values); results remain significant at q < 0.05 for four features. E-value thresholds (2 and 3) are conventional, not data-driven.
\end{itemize}
The code documentation correctly labels these estimates as ``exploratory observational exposure audits'' and states they are ``not intervention recommendations.''

\subsection{Why Recourse Was Not Generated}
The decision not to generate counterfactual recommendations is itself a contribution. Many clinical AI systems produce ``what-if'' scenarios without validating whether the suggested changes are (a)~clinically feasible, (b)~causally sound, or (c)~safe. Our deliberate omission demonstrates that responsible AI development sometimes means acknowledging the limits of available data and methodology.

\subsection{Subgroup Performance Disparities}
The declining AUC for older age groups (0.765 for ages 10--20 vs. 0.585 for ages 90--100) suggests that the model's feature set captures readmission risk factors better for younger patients. This may reflect the greater homogeneity of readmission causes in younger diabetic patients versus the multi-morbidity complexity in elderly populations.

% ══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════
% VII. LIMITATIONS
% ══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════
\section{Limitations}

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*, label=\textbf{L\arabic*.}]
    \item \textbf{Unverifiable unconfoundedness:} The AIPW estimator requires all major confounders to be measured. Unmeasured factors (physician judgment, patient adherence, social determinants) may bias estimates. E-values quantify but cannot eliminate this risk.
    \item \textbf{Single dataset, historical period (1999--2008):} Results are specific to the UCI Diabetes 130-US Hospitals dataset. There is no hospital identifier for cluster adjustment at the facility level, and the cohort predates modern diabetes management. Generalisability is limited.
    \item \textbf{Author-defined actionability policy:} The actionability gate is operationalised by the authors, not by clinicians or patients. A different policy could allow different features and change which recourse recommendations, if any, are emitted.
    \item \textbf{Administrative data:} The dataset lacks clinical notes, imaging, vitals, and social determinants, limiting both prediction quality and causal inference validity.
    \item \textbf{Binary exposure:} Continuous features are binarised at the median, losing dose-response nuance.
    \item \textbf{Modest discrimination:} AUC of 0.664 limits the clinical utility of the prediction model itself.
    \item \textbf{No actionable recourse generated:} No feature was both actionability-approved and ROBUST. While this is a safety feature, it means the pipeline does not currently produce actionable ``what-if'' scenarios.
    \item \textbf{Restricted overlap populations:} Common-support trimming changes the target population and can leave small audit samples, as seen for \texttt{number\_emergency} (38.8\% retained).
\end{enumerate}

% ══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════
% VIII. FUTURE WORK
% ══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════
\section{Future Work}

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*, nosep, label=\textbf{F\arabic*.}]
    \item \textbf{Clinician actionability catalogue:} Partner with domain experts to define which features are clinically modifiable and under what constraints, enabling the recourse layer to emit validated ``what-if'' scenarios.
    \item \textbf{Interactive dashboard:} Build a Streamlit or similar interface for clinician-facing exploration of predictions, SHAP explanations, causal audit results, and recourse scenarios.
    \item \textbf{Causal discovery:} Apply PC, FCI, or DirectLiNGAM algorithms to learn the causal graph from data, moving beyond assumed confounder sets.
    \item \textbf{Richer data:} Integrate clinical notes (via NLP), lab values, and social determinants for stronger confounding adjustment.
    \item \textbf{External validation:} Test on MIMIC-III/IV for generalizability.
    \item \textbf{Continuous treatments:} Nonparametric dose-response causal estimation to move beyond binary exposure binarisation.
    \item \textbf{Prospective evaluation:} Clinical A/B testing comparing standard vs. causally-audited explanations.
\end{enumerate}

% IX. CONCLUSION
% ══════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════
\section{Conclusion}

We presented \textbf{CausalShield}, a reproducible pipeline that pairs clinical readmission prediction with SHAP-guided causal sensitivity analysis. Applied to 99,343 diabetic patient encounters from 130 US hospitals, the pipeline demonstrates:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*, nosep]
    \item An XGBoost classifier (selected by 5-fold patient-grouped cross-validation over five candidates) achieving AUC-ROC of 0.664 (95\% CI: 0.649--0.678) on a properly patient-grouped held-out test set, with TreeSHAP identifying \texttt{number\_inpatient} (mean $|\phi| = 0.323$) as the dominant feature.
    \item An AIPW audit of five temporally eligible SHAP-ranked features (ICD diagnosis groups excluded as they are coded at discharge) with common-support trimming, BH-corrected p-values, post-weighting SMD, and E-value sensitivity analysis. SHAP directions agree with AIPW for all five features. Four associations are FRAGILE ($E \leq 2$); only \texttt{number\_inpatient} reaches UNCERTAIN ($E = 2.90$). No feature is ROBUST --- a gap that SHAP alone cannot reveal.
    \item An actionability-first recourse gate that emitted no recommendation under either the author-defined or a counterfactual ``medications allowed'' policy, because no feature was both policy-approved and ROBUST, demonstrating that responsible AI must require both statistical robustness and clinical feasibility.
    \item A held-out subgroup audit revealing substantial age-dependent variation in discrimination (AUC 0.585--0.765). Selection-rate ratios are reported at a common top-20\% operating point (threshold 0.146) and are descriptive rather than fairness conclusions.
\end{itemize}

By integrating SHAP explanations, doubly robust causal estimation, E-value sensitivity analysis, and a constrained recourse safety gate into a single reproducible pipeline, CausalShield takes a concrete step toward trustworthy clinical AI. The key remaining challenge is partnering with clinical domain experts to define actionable feature constraints that would allow the recourse layer to emit validated ``what-if'' scenarios.
